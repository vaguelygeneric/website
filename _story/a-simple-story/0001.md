Priya had exactly one idea for a story, and the idea was this: a person sits down to write a story.

She recognized, even as she typed the front matter, that this was not a strong idea. It was, at best, an idea-shaped placeholder for an idea, the literary equivalent of the little gray box that shows up when an image fails to load. But it was ten-thirty at night, the cursor was blinking in a file called `0001.md`, and blinking cursors do not respect the difference between a good idea and a idea-shaped placeholder. They just want to be filled.

"Okay," she said, to no one, because she lived alone and had long ago stopped feeling strange about narrating her own text editor usage out loud. "Front matter first. That's the professional move."

She typed:

```
---
story: a-simple-story
title: "Chapter One"
---
```

Then she stared at it for a while, the way you stare at a form at the DMV that asks for information you technically know but cannot, under observation, immediately produce. What was today's date. What time was it, precisely, down to the second, because apparently that mattered now - she'd read the README, some of it, the parts before her eyes had started sliding off the technical bits like rain off a windshield. Something about a field called `created` and a field called `publish_date`, and a stern little paragraph explaining that if she got them backward, a robot several layers down in the build pipeline would silently, wordlessly, without so much as a warning label, decide her chapter simply did not exist yet.

"That's rude," she told the README, and meant it a little.

She typed `created:` and then sat with her hands hovering over the keyboard the way a diver hesitates at the edge of a very cold pool. This was the record of when the thing had actually happened - when she, Priya, a real person with a body and a slowly cooling cup of tea, had sat down and made a thing. It felt important to get right, in the same disproportionate way it feels important to get a signature right on a form that will never be read by a human being.

`2026-07-10T20:14:00`, she wrote, which was, as far as she could tell by glancing at the corner of her screen, approximately true.

`publish_date:` came next, and this one she understood a little better, because the README had used a phrase she liked: *this is the one that actually matters publicly.* Not when she'd done the thing. When the world was allowed to know about it. There was something almost dignified about the distinction - a private truth and a public one, filed separately, on purpose.

`status: draft`, she added, because whatever this was, it was not ready to be anyone's problem but hers.

She looked at what she had. Four lines of YAML, a title she wasn't sure about, and a story that was, canonically, at this exact moment, about nothing except a woman writing four lines of YAML.

"It's a start," she said, to the blinking cursor, which did not disagree.
